Fusion Gold: Mercury to Gold

A fusion plant has to multiply its neutrons to breed fuel. If the multiplier is mercury-198, every multiplication leaves one atom of gold behind, so the gold costs no extra neutrons.

I.

What is actually being claimed?

In July 2025 a small Bay Area startup, Marathon Fusion, posted a preprint with an old title: Scalable Chrysopoeia. Chrysopoeia is the alchemists' word for making gold. The headline numbers are about 2 tonnes of gold per gigawatt of fusion heat per year, made from mercury, and enough extra revenue to roughly double what a fusion plant earns[1].

The claim is less magical than the press made it sound. Every deuterium–tritium fusion plant has to wrap its plasma in a blanket that turns each fusion neutron into slightly more than one new tritium atom. That arithmetic only works if a neutron multiplier, a material that takes in one fast neutron and gives out two, sits next to the plasma. If the multiplier is mercury-198, each multiplication event also turns one mercury atom into gold. The plant needed that reaction anyway, so the gold comes along without costing it any neutrons.

I'm going to build this up from four facts of nuclear physics, then test it against the strongest objections. The physics holds up. The business case depends on one number nobody has demonstrated: what it costs to enrich hundreds of tonnes of mercury in a single isotope. By Section VIII you'll be able to compute the break-even value of that number yourself. Numbers I derived are labelled with a confidence level; numbers from Marathon or the press are cited.

II.

Why does it have to be mass 197?

Four facts are enough to build the whole idea.

1. An element is defined by its proton count, Z, and chemistry can't change it. Mercury has Z = 80 and gold has Z = 79. Heat, acids and amalgams only rearrange electrons, with energies of a few electron-volts. Changing Z means changing the nucleus, which takes millions of electron-volts (MeV). That factor of about a million is why alchemy failed.

2. Nuclei with the same mass number A (called isobars) decay toward the most stable arrangement for that A. A nucleus with too many neutrons undergoes β− decay (n → p), so Z goes up by 1. A nucleus with too many protons undergoes electron capture (p → n), so Z goes down by 1. Neither process changes A.

3. Gold has exactly one stable isotope: Au-197 (79 protons, 118 neutrons).

4. Mercury has seven stable isotopes: 196, 198, 199, 200, 201, 202 and 204. Their natural abundances are about 0.15%, 10.0%, 16.9%, 23.1%, 13.2%, 29.9% and 6.9%. There is no stable Hg-197.

Put facts 2–4 together. Hg-197 has one proton too many for A = 197, so it captures an electron and becomes Au-197, with a half-life of about 64 hours. The whole problem therefore reduces to making Hg-197 cheaply and in bulk. Mercury has A = 196 and A = 198 but no A = 197, so you can either add a neutron to Hg-196 or remove one from Hg-198.

The obvious-looking shortcut is to knock a proton straight out with an (n,p) reaction, turning mercury directly into gold. Try it below and you'll see why it fails.

The landscape picture makes this intuitive. Along each A column there is a single lowest point, the stable isobar. From A = 198 to A = 204 that point is mercury, so any gold made at those masses is neutron-rich and decays back up into mercury. At A = 197 the lowest point is gold, so any Hg-197 you make decays down into it. A = 197 is the only column where gold is the lowest point.

Well, actually 🧐 — "decays back to mercury" has one exception, and it isn't gold either

Hg-196 (n,p) gives Au-196, which is proton-rich for A = 196 (both Pt-196 and Hg-196 are stable). It decays mostly (about 93%) by electron capture to Pt-196 and only about 7% by β− to Hg-196. So it becomes platinum, which still isn't gold. The general rule holds: away from A = 197 the decays lead somewhere other than stable gold.

A second subtlety: the old proposal of thermal capture on Hg-196 does work physically, and gold made in fission reactors this way has been reported for decades. It fails on feedstock, not physics. Hg-196 is 0.15% of mercury, 66 times scarcer than Hg-198, and its capture product Hg-197 has its own large capture cross-section, so some of it is lost to further captures before it can decay.

III.

Why can't a fission reactor do it?

Removing a neutron from Hg-198 costs energy: its neutron separation energy, about 8.5 MeV (moderate-high confidence). The incoming neutron has to supply that energy, plus a little extra for momentum conservation. Below the threshold the (n,2n) cross-section is exactly zero. No number of slow neutrons will do it.

Fission neutrons are born with a broad spread of energies, the Watt spectrum, which averages about 2 MeV. D-T fusion neutrons are all born at the same energy:

D + T → 4He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV)    Q = 17.6 MeV every fusion neutron is 5.6 MeV above the Hg-198 (n,2n) threshold

There is a second, subtler reason heavy metals make good multipliers. When a neutron bounces elastically off a nucleus of mass number A, the largest fraction of its energy it can lose in one collision is:

ΔEmax / E = 1 − ((A−1)/(A+1))2 = 4A / (A+1)2 head-on elastic collision, non-relativistic

For hydrogen (A = 1) that's 100%: a billiard ball hitting a billiard ball can stop dead. For mercury (A ≈ 198) it's 4×198/1992 ≈ 2%, like a ping-pong ball bouncing off a bowling ball. A 14 MeV neutron can bounce elastically around a thick mercury layer many times and stay above 8.5 MeV, and every bounce is another chance at (n,2n). At 14 MeV, (n,2n) is also the largest non-elastic channel for heavy nuclei, which is why lead has long been the standard heavy-metal multiplier. Marathon's related work uses σ(n,2n) ≈ 2 barns for Hg-198[2].

Well, actually 🧐 — the bowling-ball picture covers elastic scattering only

The 2% bound applies to elastic collisions. At 14 MeV, heavy nuclei also scatter inelastically: the neutron leaves the target nucleus in an excited state and can lose several MeV at once. Inelastic scattering on mercury is a real loss channel that the elastic intuition hides, and it's one reason the realistic conversion fraction is about 55–80% and not close to 100%. The intuition is still right in direction: a heavy multiplier slows neutrons far less per collision than beryllium (A = 9, up to 36% per elastic hit) and far, far less than water.

IV.

Why does a fusion plant need a neutron multiplier anyway?

This is the step that turns a physics curiosity into an engineering argument. Tritium has a 12.3-year half-life, so essentially none exists in nature. World civilian stocks are on the order of tens of kilograms, mostly a byproduct of Canadian CANDU reactors (moderate confidence). Here is how much one plant burns:

1 GW ÷ (17.6 MeV × 1.602×10−13 J/MeV) = 3.55×1020 fusions/s
× 3.156×107 s/yr = 1.12×1028 fusions/yr = 18,600 mol/yr
× 3.016 g/mol ≈ 56 kg of tritium per GW·yr of fusion power my calculation; high confidence. Each fusion consumes one triton and releases one neutron.

A single 1 GW plant burns through roughly the world's civilian stock every year or two. It must breed its own fuel, and the only practical way is lithium-6:

6Li + n → T + 4He + 4.8 MeV about 940 barns for slow (thermal) neutrons; the cross-section falls roughly as 1/velocity as the neutron speeds up

Now count. Each fusion consumes one triton and releases one neutron, and each neutron can make at most one triton. Some neutrons are always lost: absorbed in structural steel, escaping through heating and diagnostic ports, or leaking out. With no help, the tritium breeding ratio (TBR) is below 1 and the plant slowly runs out of fuel. In practice you need TBR comfortably above 1, typically 1.05–1.15 or more. The margin covers tritium decay and processing losses, keeps an inventory on hand, and produces the surplus that starts the next plant.

The standard fix is a neutron multiplier between the plasma and the lithium: beryllium (Be-9 (n,2n)) or lead (Pb (n,2n)). So the (n,2n) reaction is already in every credible D-T blanket design. The question Marathon asked is which nucleus should perform it.

V.

What happens when the multiplier is mercury?

Combine the last three sections. A multiplier performs (n,2n). Hg-198 (n,2n) is exactly the reaction that makes gold. So swap beryllium or lead for mercury enriched in Hg-198, and every neutron multiplication also produces one gold atom. The preprint proposes two blanket layers with different working fluids, each matched to the neutron energies it sees[3]:

Plasma
D + T → He + n (14.1 MeV)
Inner blanket — Hg enriched to ~90% Hg-198 (fast spectrum)
n (14 MeV) + Hg-198 → Hg-197 + 2n (a few MeV each)
Hg-197 →(64 h, electron capture)→ Au-197 ✓
Outer blanket — Li enriched to ~90% Li-6 (slower spectrum)
n + Li-6 → T + He  → tritium back to the plasma ✓
Heat exchanger
heat from every layer → turbine → electricity ✓

The order of the layers follows from the cross-sections. (n,2n) needs neutrons above 8.5 MeV, and neutrons are fastest right next to the plasma, so mercury goes inside. Li-6 capture prefers slow neutrons (roughly 1/v), and neutrons have slowed down by the time they reach the outer layer, so lithium goes outside.

Here is the move that makes the gold free, in neutron terms. Write the TBR in a toy model: a fraction f of source neutrons undergo Hg-198 (n,2n), and every neutron, multiplied or not, has probability p of being captured by Li-6:

TBR = p·(1 − f) + p·2f = p·(1 + f)
gold per GW·yr = f × 3.66 t toy model, mine; it ignores parasitic capture in mercury and inelastic losses

Why the mercury must be enriched

Natural mercury is 90% isotopes other than Hg-198. Some of them, especially Hg-199 (thermal capture about 2,000 barns, moderate confidence), are greedy absorbers of slow neutrons. They would take the neutrons Li-6 needs and make no gold in return. The preprint's simulated case enriches all the mercury to 90 at% Hg-198 and all the lithium to 90 at% Li-6. An independent OpenMC run posted on a fusion forum came to the same conclusion from the other direction: a natural-Hg, natural-Li blanket fails, with TBR dropping below 1.2 once mercury is more than about 20% of the Hg+Li mix[4]. Enrichment is not an optimisation. The whole concept depends on it. Keep that in mind for Section VIII.

Well, actually 🧐 — the multiplier eats energy, and mercury eats more than beryllium

(n,2n) is endothermic. Each Hg-198 multiplication consumes about 8.5 MeV of neutron kinetic energy, which is stored as nuclear binding energy and never becomes heat. Capturing the extra neutron in Li-6 returns 4.8 MeV, so each event costs a net ~3.7 MeV of heat, before a small refund when Hg-197 decays.

First-order ledger (my estimate, low-moderate confidence): at f = 0.55 and p = 0.85, that is about 2 MeV lost from roughly 22 MeV of heat per fusion, about 9%. Lead's (n,2n) costs about 7.4 MeV, similar to mercury. Beryllium's costs only about 1.7 MeV. So the claim that gold comes "without hurting electricity output" is believable compared with a lead blanket, but likely costs on the order of 5–15% of blanket heat compared with a beryllium blanket. A full neutronics model with gamma heating could narrow that range; I don't have one.

VI.

How much gold, and how radioactive?

The ceiling (my calculation, high confidence): one source neutron per fusion and at most one gold atom per source neutron give 18,600 mol × 197 g/mol = 3.66 tonnes of gold per GW·yr. No blanket design can exceed this, because a multiplied neutron has already lost too much energy to trigger a second (n,2n).

Marathon's number: about 2 t/GWth/yr, so f ≈ 0.55. In one configuration the preprint reports a yearly Au-197 / Hg-198 ratio of 0.0047 on a 622-tonne Hg-198 inventory. That is about 2.9 t/yr, or roughly 80% of the ceiling. Given the heavy-nucleus physics in Section III, 55–80% in a thick, highly enriched layer is physically plausible (moderate confidence).

Units in the press coverage: the Financial Times reported 5,000 kg of gold per year per gigawatt of electricity[5]. At about 40% thermal-to-electric efficiency, 1 GWe ≈ 2.5 GWth, and 2.5 × 2 t ≈ 5 t. The two figures agree once you convert units.

Inventory: burning 0.47% of the Hg-198 per year means the 622 t charge lasts on the order of 200 years. The Hg-198 is capital equipment, not a consumable. The economics depend on the upfront enrichment bill, not on ongoing feedstock.

The radioactivity catch

Fast neutrons also cause side reactions on the other mercury isotopes, producing radioactive gold: Au-196 (6.2 d), Au-198 (2.7 d), Au-199 (3.1 d), and the long tail, Au-195 (186 d), which mostly comes from Hg-196 (n,2n) Hg-195 → Au-195. Marathon's conservative release criterion is that the gold must be no more radioactive than a banana, which works out to a cooling time of about 17.7 years[6].

17.7 yr ÷ 0.51 yr (Au-195 half-life) ≈ 35 half-lives  →  2−35 ≈ 3×10−11 my sanity check, moderate confidence that Au-195 sets the tail. Eleven orders of magnitude is the right scale for going from "freshly irradiated" to "banana".

Getting the gold out is the easy part. Gold dissolves in mercury to form an amalgam, and gold miners have separated the two for centuries by distilling off the mercury (boiling point 357 °C) and leaving the gold behind. Of everything in this scheme, the extraction chemistry is the least risky step.

VII.

What is a neutron worth?

Marathon's site gives a GW-scale plant about $223M/yr from electricity alone and $482M/yr from electricity plus gold, which implies about $259M/yr from gold[7]. Here are the assumptions reverse-engineered (my inference, moderate confidence): 1 GWth × 40% efficiency × 90% capacity factor = 3.15 TWh/yr, and at about $70/MWh that comes to ~$221M, almost exactly their $223M. So "GW-scale" means 1 GW thermal. Then $259M ÷ 2–2.4 t implies a gold price of about $110–130k/kg, roughly the range when the paper was written.

Gold has risen since. Spot traded around $4,140–4,200/oz in October 2026[8], about $134k/kg (1 kg = 32.15 troy oz). At that price, 2 t is worth about $268M/yr and 2.9 t about $390M/yr. So the gold line would now be larger than the electricity line.

Why gold, and not something pricier?

Think of neutrons as the product and ask what to sell them as. Medical isotopes such as actinium-225 are worth vastly more per atom, but the entire world market is grams to kilograms per year. A 1 GW plant releases about 1028 neutrons a year and would flood that market almost immediately. Gold is worth only about $4.4×10−20 per atom (197 g/mol × $134/g ÷ 6.022×1023), but it has an above-ground stock worth over $20 trillion and a deep, liquid market that absorbs tonnes without noticing.

The actual commercial insight

Gold is the rare product that is valuable per atom and also has effectively unlimited demand at power-plant volumes. Isotopes worth more per atom have markets too small to absorb a plant's output, and commodities with deep enough markets are worth too little per atom. Gold sits at the intersection.

Well, actually 🧐 — one widely shared revenue figure is off by ~35×

A trade outlet wrote that a reactor producing 5,000 kg of gold a year would earn "only $17 million" from it[9]. Check it: 5,000 kg × 32.15 oz/kg × $3,400/oz (the price the same article quotes) ≈ $547M. At October 2026 prices it's about $670M. Ignore the $17M figure; it's an arithmetic error, not an alternative estimate.

VIII.

Which number decides whether it pays?

Start with mass balance. Making 622 t of 90% Hg-198 from natural mercury (~10% Hg-198) uses the standard enrichment feed equation:

Feed = Product × (xp − xt) / (xf − xt)
tails 2%: 622 × 0.88 / 0.08 ≈ 6,900 t of natural mercury
tails 5%: 622 × 0.85 / 0.05 ≈ 10,600 t x = Hg-198 fraction in product (p), feed (f), tails (t); my calculation

That's per plant. Global primary mercury production is a few thousand tonnes a year (low-moderate confidence), and the Minamata Convention is phasing primary mining out. There is one point in Marathon's favour: thousands of tonnes of mercury are sitting in stockpiles from decommissioned chlor-alkali plants, waiting for disposal. That stock could be cheap feed, perhaps even negatively priced. Still, no heavy element other than uranium has ever been isotopically separated at the thousand-tonne scale. The preprint itself says mercury enrichment must scale from laboratory demonstrations to industrial levels, and gives only preliminary cost estimates for photochemical enrichment.

So treat the enrichment cost as the unknown, and find the value at which the gold stream just pays for the inventory. Gold sales start only after the plant runs and the first batch cools for ~18 years, so discounting does most of the work:

PVgold = R × [1 − (1+r)−40] / r × (1+r)−D
break-even enrichment cost = PVgold ÷ 622,000 kg R = annual gold revenue, r = discount rate, D = years from building the inventory to the first sale, 40 years of sales

Well, actually 🧐 — a uranium-style SWU estimate lands above break-even

Enrichment effort is measured in separative work units (SWU), using the value function V(x) = (2x − 1)·ln(x/(1−x)). For 1 kg of 90% product from 9.97% feed with 2% tails, you feed 11.0 kg, reject 10.0 kg, and spend:

SWU = P·V(0.90) + T·V(0.02) − F·V(0.0997) ≈ 1.76 + 37.5 − 19.4 ≈ ~20 SWU per kg

Uranium centrifuge enrichment sells for very roughly $100–150 per SWU. If mercury could be enriched at that price, the inventory would cost about $2,000–3,000/kg, or $1.2–1.9B: above the ~$1,300/kg break-even. Treat this with low confidence. SWU prices don't carry over between elements; mercury is a seven-isotope mix, not a binary one; and the mass difference between neighbouring Hg isotopes is small. The more promising route is photochemical separation, which exploits the isotope shift of mercury's 253.7 nm line and was developed in the 1980s–90s to enrich Hg-196 for fluorescent lamps. That technology has never come close to thousands of tonnes.

Two adjustments favour Marathon. The enriched mercury is not consumed: after 40 years most of the 622 t is still there and could be resold or moved to the next plant. And a plant without mercury still has to buy a beryllium or lead multiplier. Only the difference in cost should be charged against the gold.

IX.

What are the strongest objections?

1. There is no D-T power plant (high confidence). ITER's 2024 rebaseline puts full D-T operation around 2039[10]. Commercial tokamaks such as CFS's ARC are aiming for the 2030s, and fusion schedules have a long record of slipping. A plant switched on in 2040 sells its first cooled gold around 2058. At 8%, a dollar arriving in year 18 is worth about $0.25 today. "Doubling revenue" sounds much bigger in nominal dollars than in present value.

2. Enrichment is the crux (high confidence that it's the crux; low confidence on its cost). See Section VIII. Of all the inputs, enrichment cost is the one that moves the answer most and is the least known.

3. Liquid mercury inside a tokamak is a hard engineering problem in its own right (moderate-high confidence). Mercury is electrically conductive, so pumping it across magnetic fields of several tesla creates large magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) drag and pressure drops, the same issue that troubles lead-lithium blankets. At 13.5 g/cm³, the inventory is hundreds of tonnes of structural load. Mercury also has a significant vapour pressure, is toxic, and becomes radioactive under neutron bombardment. None of these is a showstopper on its own, but each one makes the plant more expensive.

4. The gold dividend shrinks as fusion succeeds (moderate confidence). Mine supply is about 3,600 t/yr. A 1 GWth plant adds ~2 t, which is noise. But 1,000 GWth of these plants would add ~2,000 t/yr, a +55% supply shock, and the price would fall. You can read this as a feature: the subsidy is largest for the first few dozen plants, which need it most, and fades once fusion can stand on its own. Either way, you can't model a fleet at $4,000/oz.

5. Market acceptance is unknown. Once cooled, fusion gold is atomically identical to mined gold, with no detectable signature like the one that separates lab-grown diamonds from mined ones. But regulators have to clear free release of material that was once radioactive, and some buyers may discount it. I have no strong evidence either way (unknown).

6. Another reading: gold is the marketing story and tritium is the real one. On Marathon's own page, the first benefit listed is excess tritium to start future plants, with gold as a "natural dividend." The binding constraint on how fast a fusion fleet can grow is the tritium doubling time: how quickly plant N produces the startup inventory for plant N+1. A better multiplier shortens that time. Gold gets the headlines, but tritium surplus may matter more strategically. I think this is the correct reading.

X.

What does Marathon Fusion actually do today?

Marathon doesn't build tokamaks. My reading (inference, moderate confidence) is that the gold concept is IP and blanket design they hope to license to power-plant developers. Their current work rests on the same principle as the gold scheme: 14 MeV neutrons enable (n,2n) chemistry that fission reactors can't.

  • Near-term machine: a small beam-target neutron source. An RF plasma produces deuterium ions, which are accelerated to keV energies into a lithium target that holds tritium as lithium tritide and can be regenerated in place. Listed specs are about 1 kW of fusion power and 1012 n/cm²/s. Check: 1 kW ÷ 17.6 MeV ≈ 3.5×1014 neutrons/s, consistent with that flux close to the target.
  • Radiopharmaceuticals: Ra-226 (n,2n) Ra-225, which then β− decays (14.9 d) to Ac-225, a prized cancer-therapy isotope. This is the gold route in reverse: (n,2n) removes a neutron, then a decay changes Z, but this time Z goes up. Medical isotopes are a market where a small neutron source can earn money today.
  • Fuel cycle: tritium processing, and Li-6 enrichment with a plasma centrifuge.
  • Funding: about $6M in investment plus $4M in government grants as of mid-2025 (not checked for later rounds)[11].

The strategy, then: isotope revenue now, fuel-cycle components for fusion developers next, and gold as a long-dated option on fusion working. That's a sensible structure for a hardware startup. But the headline, mercury to gold, is the most distant and most capital-hungry part of the company.

XI.

Can you do the arithmetic yourself?

Reading this isn't the same as understanding it. Try each exercise before opening the answer.

E1 — isobar logic. A colleague proposes Hg-200 (n,p) Au-200. What does Au-200 do next, and why? Then explain why Hg-196 (n,2n) Hg-195 doesn't give stable gold either.

Answer E1

At A = 200 the stable isobar is Hg-200, so Au-200 is neutron-rich and β− decays (48 min) straight back to Hg-200: the net effect is nothing. Hg-195 is proton-rich and decays by electron capture to Au-195. But A = 195 isn't gold's basin. Pt-195 is the stable isobar, so Au-195 keeps decaying (186 d) to platinum. You get a radioactive gold contaminant and then platinum, not stable gold.

E2 — neutron counting. A smaller plant runs at 400 MW of fusion power. Compute (a) neutrons per year, (b) the maximum possible gold per year, and (c) gold per year if 60% of source neutrons do Hg-198 (n,2n).

Answer E2

(a) 0.4 × 1.12×1028 ≈ 4.5×1027 neutrons/yr (≈ 7,440 mol). (b) 7,440 × 197 g ≈ 1.47 t. (c) 0.6 × 1.47 ≈ 0.88 t/yr.

E3 — neutron budget. Using TBR = p(1+f) with p = 0.85, what minimum f gives TBR ≥ 1.10? What does that say about the trade-off (or lack of one) between gold and tritium?

Answer E3

1.10 / 0.85 − 1 ≈ 0.29. You need about 29% of source neutrons doing (n,2n) just to be fuel self-sufficient with margin. Every extra unit of f raises tritium and gold together, so there is no trade-off inside this model. The real trade-offs sit outside it: parasitic capture in mercury, energy lost to an endothermic reaction (Section V sidenote), and the enrichment bill.

E4 — enrichment economics. How much natural mercury do you need for 622 t at 90% Hg-198 with 1% tails? With $268M/yr of gold starting 18 years out, 8% discount rate and 40 years of sales, at what enrichment cost X ($/kg) does the gold's present value equal the inventory cost?

Answer E4

Feed = 622 × (0.90 − 0.01) / (0.0997 − 0.01) ≈ 6,170 t. The annuity factor (8%, 40 yr) is 11.92, and the discount factor 1.08−18 is 0.250, so PV ≈ 268 × 11.92 × 0.250 ≈ $800M. X ≈ $800M / 622,000 kg ≈ $1,290/kg. Widget 4 reproduces this.

E5 — market saturation. How many GWth of these plants would raise annual gold supply by 10%? If gold's demand elasticity is −0.5 (assume it for this exercise), what happens to the price, and to each plant's gold revenue?

Answer E5

10% of ~3,600 t = 360 t ÷ 2 t/GW ≈ 180 GWth. Price falls by about 10% / 0.5 = 20%, and each plant's gold revenue falls by the same 20%. That's a crude constant-elasticity approximation; gold demand is dominated by investors and central banks, so the real response could be smaller or larger. Either way, the first ~50 plants barely move the price, and a fleet of a few hundred does.

E6 — design reasoning. Explain why putting the lithium layer inside the mercury layer, next to the plasma, would sharply cut gold production.

Answer E6

Neutrons are above the 8.5 MeV threshold only near the plasma. Put lithium there and two things go wrong. First, its light nuclei slow neutrons quickly (Li-7 can take up to ~44% of a neutron's energy in one elastic hit, against 2% for mercury), so most neutrons fall below 8.5 MeV before they ever reach the mercury, and f collapses. Second, Li-6's capture cross-section at 14 MeV is tiny because it falls roughly as 1/v, so the lithium breeds poorly in exactly the place you put it. You lose the gold, you lose the multiplication, and the lithium still has to do its breeding further out. The threshold and the 1/v law together force the order.

What you can now teach

A D-T fusion plant must multiply its neutrons with (n,2n) reactions to breed enough tritium. If the multiplier is enriched mercury-198, each multiplication makes Hg-197, which decays into gold-197, so gold and tritium come out of the same reaction. The physics works because only A = 197 drains into gold and only 14 MeV fusion neutrons clear the 8.5 MeV threshold at scale. The business case depends on one unproven number: whether hundreds of tonnes of mercury can be enriched for less than about $1,300/kg.
